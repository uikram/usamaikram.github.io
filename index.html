Usama Ikram

I am a graduate researcher in artificial intelligence, working in machine learning and computer vision with a growing focus on multimodal and foundation models. My research asks what happens to pretrained models when they leave the benchmark: how they can be adapted to specialised domains at reasonable cost, and whether the resulting systems are dependable for the purpose they were built for.

That question has taken me across several settings. I have derived a latency bound that decides whether a vision-language model can sit inside a real-time human-robot interaction loop, adapted lightweight vision backbones to holographic cell imaging where the usual training objectives quietly fail, built a network that measures cells directly from a raw hologram and reported plainly where its central idea did not work, and led a systematic review that counted how much evidence stands behind a set of clinical claims. Before my MS I spent two years as an AI research engineer, building models that had to run on constrained hardware and on data that rarely behaved as documented.

I have three first-authored manuscripts under review and a fourth in preparation, and I am applying for PhD positions starting in the second half of 2027.

Research interests

Broadly, machine learning and computer vision, with particular interest in the areas below. Some of these I have worked in directly; others are directions I want to develop into during a PhD, and I have tried to keep that distinction visible.

Multimodal and foundation models

Vision-language models are where my hands-on experience sits, mainly on the adaptation and deployment side. I would like to move further into systems that combine vision, language and other modalities, and into how large generative models can support systems that cannot tolerate their latency.

What adaptation preserves, and what it deletes

Low-rank adaptation of pretrained backbones, and what a restricted adaptation subspace changes about what a model retains. In one study it preserved rare classes that full fine-tuning removed, at no compute advantage; in another it left the representation intact while changing zero-shot behaviour.

Evaluation on the quantity that matters

Judging a model by what its output is used for: per-class rather than average scores, per-cell measurement error rather than image similarity, worst-case rather than mean latency, and differences counted only when they exceed run-to-run noise.

Real-time and deployable AI

What models cost to run and what that cost implies for the systems around them: latency, memory and energy characterisation, graph compilation and quantisation, and embedded inference.

Healthcare and biomedical imaging

Most of my applied work has been medical or biological, where labelled data are scarce, the rare class is often the decisive one, and a measurement without an error bar is of limited use. Uncertainty quantification is a direction I want to develop into; I have not worked on it yet.

Delay-Aware Deployability of Multimodal Foundation Models for Real-Time Human-Robot Interaction

Usama Ikram, Youhyun Kim, Inkyu Moon

Submitted to IEEE Transactions on Human-Machine Systems Under review

Vision-language models are increasingly proposed as perception modules for assistive systems, and are compared almost entirely on accuracy. In a wearable interaction loop the model's inference time is a transport delay, and past some threshold the loop becomes unstable however accurate the perception is. This work derives that threshold: modelling the loop as a PID-controlled mass-spring-damper plant with perception delay, a first-order Padé approximation and the Routh-Hurwitz criterion give a stability bound of 37.77 ms, against which three architectures are compared on worst-case latency.

  • LoRA-adapted CLIP (0.65% of parameters trained) kept linear-probe quality at 85.69% against 85.95%, while zero-shot transfer rose 6.05% on remote-sensing imagery and fell 4.37% on object-centric data: the features survived and the zero-shot readout moved.
  • Merged LoRA-CLIP reached 17.92 ms worst-case latency, inside the bound and inside the 18.76 ms bound that survives ±50% biomechanical variation. Leaving the adapters unmerged added 8.5 ms.
  • A Frozen Prefix-LM reached 442.02 ms, 11.7× over the bound, although its visual encoder alone took 8.25 ms. The cost is autoregressive decoding, not perception.

Measured on a desktop RTX A5000; edge energy and battery figures are projections, and the analysis was validated in software-in-the-loop simulation rather than on a physical robot.

Physics-Aware Lightweight Segmentation for Quantitative Holographic Cell Analysis with Optical Mass Preservation

Usama Ikram, Youhyun Kim, Inkyu Moon

Submitted to Applied Physics B: Lasers and Optics Under review

In holographic microscopy a cell's dry mass is its quantitative phase integrated over the segmented region, so the predicted boundary is the domain of a measurement, and a pixel-overlap loss never constrains it. This work adds phase-mask contrast, boundary-gradient alignment and integrated-phase preservation terms to the objective, and uses LoRA to adapt three lightweight backbones from natural images to red-blood-cell phase maps.

  • Integrated-phase error fell from 5.22% to 3.04% on EdgeSAM and from 15.52% to 3.01% on MobileNet-UNet, with boundary F1 rising to 0.953 and 0.970. The terms interact: contrast alone collapsed boundary F1 to 0.386, and on MobileSAM the full objective did worse than a partial one.
  • Full fine-tuning drove one minority class to zero Dice in all three backbones; LoRA at rank 8 kept all four classes in both attention-based backbones. The CNN that missed two classes had the best boundary F1 of the three, which is why per-class reporting matters.
  • On 1,974 cells over a 47-day storage study, dry mass from predicted masks agreed with annotation-derived values at r = 0.966, and ONNX FP16 inference took 4.33 ms at 16.4 MB.

Efficiency figures are desktop-GPU measurements; embedded deployment is not demonstrated. Model selection used the validation set, and no independent held-out cohort was available.

Measurement-Oriented End-to-End Holographic Quantitative Phase Analysis: Joint Reconstruction, Segmentation and Per-Cell Measurement from a Single Raw Hologram

Usama Ikram, Inkyu Moon

Manuscript In preparation

The previous study could move the boundary but not the phase inside it, which came from a classical reconstruction outside the learned system. Here one network, a shared MobileNetV2 encoder with two U-Net decoders at 9.6M parameters, maps a raw hologram directly to phase, segmentation and per-cell dry mass, with no numerical reconstruction at inference. It is evaluated on the measurement, not the image, on 800 fields of three cancer cell lines imaged in both off-axis and in-line Gabor geometry, with three training seeds and differences counted only when they exceed twice the between-seed spread.

  • In the in-line geometry, where the classical pipeline as configured recovered no usable phase, the network reached a phase correlation of 0.81 over 1,755 matched cells.
  • The central hypothesis failed and is reported as such: a per-cell integrated-phase loss raised dry-mass error beyond seed noise at every weight tried. My stated but untested interpretation is that labels derived from the reconstruction target leave the loss nothing to add, and an independent membrane-stain check shows how large that concern is.
  • A model-free error budget sets the scale: a segmentation score is not a proxy for measurement quality, and the measurement chain's own floor is 0.036% per cell.

LoRA is implemented but was not trained, so no LoRA claim is made; timings (8.42 ms per field, ONNX FP16) are workstation-GPU figures, and all data come from one instrument.

Determinants of Glycaemic Outcomes in Children and Adolescents with Type 1 Diabetes: A Systematic Review and Evidence Map

Usama Ikram (corresponding author), Abey Jose, Francisca C. Eyzaguirre, Alejandro Mac Cawley

Submitted to Pediatric Diabetes · PROSPERO CRD420251045872 Under review

With collaborators at the University of Limerick and Pontificia Universidad Católica de Chile. Reviews in this area list determinants that have been shown to matter without recording how many studies tested each. I led a review of 63 studies drawn from 5,406 records across four databases, screened independently by two reviewers and reported to PRISMA 2020, and built the PEARL framework: 47 determinants across 12 sub-domains and five domains, assigned under four stated rules, each with a counted weight of evidence.

  • Evidence volume and consistency ran in opposite directions: 68% of the 148 physiological associations reached significance, against all 26 in the psychological domain, which selective reporting explains more economically than larger effects.
  • Frequently cited determinants are not always well supported: sex reached significance in 9 of 23 studies, body mass index in 7 of 15.
