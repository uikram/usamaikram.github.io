I am a graduate researcher in the Intelligent Imaging and Vision Systems Laboratory at DGIST, working on computer vision and multimodal AI. Most of my work takes large pretrained models, adapts them to a new domain with LoRA, and then checks what they keep, what they lose, and whether they hold up where they are actually used.

So far that has meant SAM-family segmentation models adapted to quantitative phase images of red blood cells, CLIP and a generative vision-language model tested against the timing limit of a real-time control loop, a network that recovers phase and cell masks directly from raw holograms in digital holographic microscopy, and a 63-study systematic review on paediatric type 1 diabetes. Before my MS, I spent two years in industry building computer vision, time-series anomaly detection, and LLM-based evaluation systems, including on-device inference on embedded hardware.

Research Interests

These are the questions I want to keep working on in a PhD. Each one comes from a result in my own manuscripts.

01

What adaptation keeps and what it loses

When I adapt a pretrained model to a small, imbalanced dataset, the average score does not tell me enough. In M2, full fine-tuning erased the echinocyte class in all three backbones, while rank-8 LoRA kept all four classes in EdgeSAM and MobileSAM. In M3, LoRA-CLIP kept its linear-probe accuracy (85.69% against 85.95%) but its zero-shot accuracy moved: up 6.05% on EuroSAT and down 4.37% on object-centric datasets.

  • Which parts of a pretrained model carry the rare classes, and can adaptation be limited so it does not overwrite them?
  • How can we tell that a rare class is collapsing when there are few or no labels for it?
LoRARare classesFoundation model adaptation
02

Models judged on the measurement

In holographic microscopy, a cell's dry mass is the phase integrated over its mask, so the mask is part of the measurement. In M1, moving every reference boundary out by one pixel changed dry mass by 4.8% while Dice stayed at 0.974. In M2, an integrated-phase term cut integrated-phase error from 5.22% to 3.04%, but in M1 similar terms made dry-mass error slightly worse when phase and mask were learned together from raw holograms.

  • When do physical constraints help a model learn, and when do they compete with the main objective?
  • How should segmentation and reconstruction models be evaluated on the quantity a biologist or clinician actually uses?
DHMQuantitative cell analysisPhysics-guided learning
03

Vision-language models that can be checked

In M3, a generative VLM gave fluent descriptions, but its visual features scored 51.25% under linear probing against 85.95% for CLIP, and its worst-case latency (442.02 ms) was far above the 37.77 ms bound of the loop. Fluent output did not mean strong visual features or usable timing. I want to work on multimodal models for health and biomedical imaging whose answers can be traced back to the image.

  • Why do generative objectives give weaker visual features than contrastive ones, and can one model keep both?
  • How can a medical VLM's statements be checked against image regions or measurements?

M1 Under review Quantitative cell analysis · Digital holographic microscopy Includes negative results Public Code

Joint Phase Reconstruction and Cell Segmentation from Raw Holograms for Per-Cell Dry-Mass Measurement

Usama Ikram, Inkyu Moon

A cell's dry mass is its phase integrated over the segmented cell, so its accuracy depends on both the reconstructed phase and the cell boundary. One 9.6M-parameter network (a MobileNetV2 encoder with two U-Net-style decoders) maps a raw hologram to a quantitative phase map and a cell mask in 8.39 ms per 900×900 field. The dataset has 800 fields of three human cancer cell lines (NCI-H1299, SNU-475 and T-24), each under a control condition or one of four drugs, with a paired off-axis and in-line Gabor hologram for every field. Off-axis test results use 113 fields (3,186 reference cells) and three training seeds.

  • In-line holograms: On the 107 test fields whose in-line Gabor hologram is correctly paired with the reference, a single in-line training run reached phase r = 0.867 and a dry-mass MAPE of 21.3%. The classical in-line reconstruction I tested recovered near-background contrast (r = −0.21).
  • Negative results: Adding integrated-phase terms to the joint objective increased matched-cell dry-mass MAPE (+0.0141 per cell, +0.0145 per image), beyond between-seed variation. A forward-model consistency term did not constrain the off-axis propagation distance, while the in-line residual had its minimum at the recording distance.
  • Parameters vs. speed: A compact decoder with 65% fewer parameters (3.36M against 9.60M) gave nearly the same dry-mass MAPE (0.1740 against 0.1742), but latency was only 6 to 7% lower in three of four runtime and precision settings, because activation memory, not weights, dominates the cost.
Scope, measurement chain & limitations

Segmentation reference masks are derived from the off-axis reference phase, so reported metrics measure agreement with the classical off-axis reference rather than an independent fluorescence ground truth. A synthetic test checked the dry-mass integration chain to within 0.036% per cell. Timings (8.39 ms per 900×900 field, ONNX FP16) are network-only measurements on an NVIDIA RTX A5000 workstation.

Physics-Aware Lightweight Segmentation for Quantitative Holographic Cell Analysis with Optical Mass Preservation

Usama Ikram, Youhyun Kim, Inkyu Moon

In quantitative phase imaging, the predicted cell contour is the region over which phase is integrated to get dry mass, yet a standard overlap loss treats every boundary pixel the same. This work adds a physics-aware loss with three terms (phase-mask contrast, boundary-gradient alignment and integrated-phase preservation) to a class-weighted multi-class Dice loss, and uses rank-8 LoRA to adapt EdgeSAM, MobileSAM and a MobileNet-UNet to 1,090 annotated red blood cell phase images with four morphology classes (discocytes, echinocytes, spherocytes and stomatocytes).

M3 Under review · IEEE Transactions on Human-Machine Systems Vision-language models · Real-time stability & representation probing Public Code

Delay-Aware Deployability of Multimodal Foundation Models for Real-Time Human-Robot Interaction

Usama Ikram, Youhyun Kim, Inkyu Moon

In a closed-loop wearable or human-robot interaction system, a foundation model's inference latency acts as a feedback transport delay τ; past a critical threshold τmax, phase margin vanishes and the loop oscillates however accurate the vision is. Using a first-order Padé approximation and the Routh-Hurwitz criterion, we derive the stability bound (τmax = 37.77 ms nominally, 18.76 ms under ±50% parameter variation) and compare zero-shot CLIP, LoRA-adapted CLIP (0.98M trainable parameters, 0.65%) and a generative Frozen Prefix-LM on latency, energy, linear-probe accuracy and zero-shot transfer.

Scope & limitations

Latency and energy (NVML) were measured on a desktop RTX A5000 GPU. Wearable battery-life figures are projections, and closed-loop stability was checked in software-in-the-loop simulation of a mass-spring-damper plant, not on physical hardware.

M4 Under review · Pediatric Diabetes Clinical evidence synthesis · PROSPERO CRD420251045872 · Corresponding author

Determinants of Glycaemic Outcomes in Children and Adolescents with Type 1 Diabetes: A Systematic Review and Evidence Map

Usama Ikram (corresponding author), Abey Jose, Francisca C. Eyzaguirre, Alejandro Mac Cawley

Glycaemic control in paediatric type 1 diabetes depends on clinical, psychological, behavioural, family and socioeconomic factors that are usually studied separately. With co-authors at the University of Limerick and Pontificia Universidad Católica de Chile, we searched PubMed, Embase, Scopus and Web of Science (5,406 records), following PRISMA 2020 with MMAT quality appraisal, and organised 63 studies and 297 factor-study associations into the PEARL framework (47 determinants, 12 sub-domains, 5 domains).

Fig. 4 · The PEARL framework Physiology (15 factors), Lifestyle and Living Environment (10), Relationships (9), Emotional Wellbeing (8) and Autonomy (5), with study counts per factor.
  • Where the evidence sits: Physiology holds 31.9% of factors (15/47) but 49.8% of tested associations (148/297). Age was tested in 32 studies (significant in 25), diabetes duration in 26 (20), sex in 23 (9) and insulin delivery in 20 (17), while nine factors rest on a single study.
  • A sign of selective reporting: The well-replicated physiological factors gave mixed results (68% significant), while every one of the 26 emotional-wellbeing associations was significant. That pattern is more consistent with selective reporting than with uniformly larger effects.
Scope & limitations

The review maps testing frequency and significance consistency across pre-specified thresholds (p < 0.05, 0.10, or 0.15) rather than meta-analysing standardized effect sizes, because statistical reporting differs too much across the 63 studies (PROSPERO CRD420251045872).
