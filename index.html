MS Candidate in Artificial Intelligence · DGIST, Republic of Korea

Usama Ikram

I am a graduate researcher in the Intelligent Imaging and Vision Systems Laboratory at DGIST, working on computer vision and multimodal AI. Most of my work takes large pretrained models, adapts them to a new domain with LoRA, and then checks what they keep, what they lose, and whether they hold up where they are actually used.

So far that has meant SAM-family segmentation models adapted to cell images, CLIP and a generative vision-language model tested inside a real-time control loop, and networks that recover phase and cell masks directly from raw holograms in digital holographic microscopy. Before my MS I spent about two years in industry building vision, time-series and LLM-based systems, some of them running on embedded hardware.

In a PhD I want to work on vision-language models and LLMs for healthcare and biomedical imaging, with a focus on explainable and trustworthy AI.

M1Under reviewQuantitative cell analysis

Joint Phase Reconstruction and Cell Segmentation from Raw Holograms for Per-Cell Dry-Mass Measurement

Usama Ikram, Inkyu Moon

A cell's dry mass is its phase integrated over the segmented cell, so it depends on the phase and the boundary together. One 9.6M-parameter network (MobileNetV2 encoder, two U-Net decoders) maps a raw hologram directly to quantitative phase and a cell segmentation, with no numerical reconstruction at inference, and is scored on paired per-cell dry mass together with detection recall and field totals. Data: 800 fields of three cancer cell lines, each with an off-axis and an in-line Gabor hologram; three training seeds with a between-seed criterion set in advance.

22.0% → 17.4%
matched-cell dry-mass error, classical pipeline to network (off-axis), at similar Dice
0.593
detection recall: 0.912 for interior cells, 0.212 at the field boundary
4.8%
dry-mass change from a one-pixel boundary shift while Dice stays 0.974
  • In-line, one training run reached phase r = 0.867 where the tested classical in-line reconstruction recovered only near-background contrast.
  • Adding measurement-aware integrated-phase losses increased the per-cell error beyond seed variation, and a hologram forward-model term did not constrain the off-axis propagation distance. Both are reported as negative results.
Scope and limitations

Segmentation labels are derived from the reference phase, so results are agreement with that reference rather than with independent ground truth. All data come from one instrument. Timings (8.39 ms per 900×900 field, ONNX FP16) are network-only, on a workstation RTX A5000.

M2Under reviewApplied Physics B: Lasers and Optics

Physics-Aware Lightweight Segmentation for Quantitative Holographic Cell Analysis with Optical Mass Preservation

Usama Ikram, Youhyun Kim, Inkyu Moon

In holographic microscopy the predicted cell boundary is the domain of a measurement, and a pixel-overlap loss never constrains it. This work adds phase-mask contrast, boundary-gradient alignment and integrated-phase preservation terms to the segmentation objective, and uses LoRA to adapt EdgeSAM, MobileSAM and a MobileNet-UNet from natural images to red-blood-cell phase maps.

5.22% → 3.04%
integrated-phase error, Dice-only to physics-aware loss (EdgeSAM)
0.000 → 0.776
rare-class (echinocyte) Dice, full fine-tuning to rank-8 LoRA
r = 0.966
predicted vs annotated dry mass, 1,974 cells over 47 days
  • On MobileNet-UNet the same loss cut integrated-phase error from 15.52% to 3.01%. The terms interact, though: phase-mask contrast on its own collapsed boundary F1 to 0.386.
  • Full fine-tuning erased a minority class in all three backbones, while LoRA kept all four classes in both SAM-based models. The CNN with the best boundary F1 (0.971) had stopped predicting both minority classes, which an average score would never show.
Scope and limitations

Efficiency figures (4.33 ms, 16.4 MB, ONNX FP16) are desktop-GPU measurements; embedded deployment is not demonstrated. Model selection used the validation set, and no independent held-out cohort was available.

M3Under reviewIEEE Transactions on Human-Machine Systems

Delay-Aware Deployability of Multimodal Foundation Models for Real-Time Human-Robot Interaction

Usama Ikram, Youhyun Kim, Inkyu Moon

In a wearable interaction loop a model's inference time acts as a transport delay, and past some threshold the loop becomes unstable however accurate the perception is. A first-order Padé approximation and the Routh-Hurwitz criterion give that threshold, 37.77 ms, and three architectures (CLIP, LoRA-adapted CLIP, a Frozen Prefix-LM) are compared on worst-case latency against it.

37.77 ms
stability bound of the loop, from a Padé approximation and Routh-Hurwitz
17.92 vs 442 ms
worst-case latency, merged LoRA-CLIP vs the Frozen Prefix-LM
51.25% vs 85.95%
linear-probe accuracy, generative VLM features vs CLIP
  • LoRA-CLIP, training 0.65% of the parameters, kept its linear-probe accuracy (85.69%) while zero-shot accuracy rose 6.05% on remote-sensing images and fell 4.37% on object-centric data. The representation held and the zero-shot readout moved.
  • The Frozen Prefix-LM gave fluent, image-grounded answers, but took 442 ms end to end although its visual encoder ran in 8.25 ms. Autoregressive decoding, not perception, put it 11.7× over the bound. Leaving the LoRA adapters unmerged added 8.5 ms.
Scope and limitations

Measured on a desktop RTX A5000; edge energy and battery figures are projections, and the analysis was validated in software-in-the-loop simulation rather than on a physical robot.

M4Under reviewPediatric Diabetes · PROSPERO CRD420251045872

Determinants of Glycaemic Outcomes in Children and Adolescents with Type 1 Diabetes: A Systematic Review and Evidence Map

Usama Ikram (corresponding author), Abey Jose, Francisca C. Eyzaguirre, Alejandro Mac Cawley

How well children and adolescents with type 1 diabetes control their blood glucose varies a lot, and the factors behind it are studied in separate clinical, psychological, behavioural, family and socioeconomic literatures that rarely meet. Earlier reviews each cover one part of this and do not say how often a factor has been tested or how consistently it holds up.

I led this review as first and corresponding author, with collaborators at the University of Limerick and Pontificia Universidad Católica de Chile. We searched Web of Science, Scopus, PubMed and Embase up to January 2026, screened records with two independent reviewers and reported to PRISMA 2020. The included studies gave 297 factor-study associations, which I organised into the PEARL framework: 47 determinants in 12 sub-domains and five domains (physiology and clinical status, emotional and psychological wellbeing, autonomy and engagement in self-care, relationships and family dynamics, and lifestyle, learning and living environment), each with a count of how often it was tested and how often it reached significance.

5,406 → 63
records screened to included studies, across four databases
47
determinants in the PEARL framework, from 297 associations
68% vs 100%
associations reaching significance: physiology (101/148) vs emotional wellbeing (26/26)
  • The evidence is uneven. Physiology held 31.9% of the factors but 49.8% of all associations, and nine factors rested on a single study.
  • The most tested factors were age (32 studies, significant in 25), duration of diabetes (26; 20), sex (23; 9) and insulin delivery method (20; 17).
  • The well-replicated biomedical findings were inconsistent, while the small psychosocial literature was uniformly significant. Selective reporting explains that more simply than larger effects, so replicating psychosocial and family factors, with effect sizes reported, is the main research priority.
Scope and limitations

The review counts how often and how consistently each factor was tested. It did not extract the direction or size of associations, so it does not say whether a factor raises or lowers glucose levels. Significance was counted at any of three pre-specified thresholds (p < 0.05, 0.10 or 0.15). PROSPERO CRD420251045872.
